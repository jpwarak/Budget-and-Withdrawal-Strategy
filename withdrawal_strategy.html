💰 Income and Budget

Your working income and your retirement spending plan.

  • Income (plus its "pay raise %/yr") and Bonus together are your pre-retirement earned income. Both feed the Accumulation phase's RRSP contribution-room tracking: this year's earned income (Income, escalated by its own pay-raise %/yr, plus Bonus, escalated by inflation since it has no raise %/yr of its own) accrues next year's new RRSP room at 18%, capped at that year's RRSP dollar limit ($33,810 for 2026, itself escalated forward), minus any Pension Adjustment entered for a DB pension you're accruing. If Income is left at $0, the app falls back to a flat $8,000/yr of new room instead (minus the Pension Adjustment), regardless of Bonus.
  • Living-expense and savings line items (rent/mortgage, property tax, and everything else) each escalate forward with your own frequency and rate settings, and feed the year-by-year spending target the retirement simulation tries to meet every year.
  • The Emergency Fund savings line is its own dedicated contribution -- see the Emergency Fund subsection under Retirement Income for how its 6-month cap works once you're retired.
  • This tab's budget is the real, granular spending number the retirement simulation actually funds -- it's what decides a real Surplus or Shortfall in the Year-by-Year table's Cash Flow column. It's separate from the "Target Income" figure on the Retirement Income tab, which only sizes registered (LIF/RRIF) withdrawals; see Target vs. Budget vs. Cash Flow under Retirement Income below. Every edit here flows straight through to the retirement simulation: a moment after you stop typing, the Accumulation projection and the live Retirement Income simulation are re-run with the new figures, so the funding badge, balances, Net Estate, Tax Breakdown and Report all reflect what you just changed by the time you switch tabs (before 2026-09-16 only the table's Budget column refreshed; the simulated figures kept using the old budget until some other control forced a re-run).
  • The Yearly Expense Summary sub-tab shows the same budget, escalated year by year out to age 85, broken down by category.
  • The "Payroll Deductions (est.)" card, added 2026-09-13, estimates EI, CPP/QPP, CPP2/QPP2, and (Quebec only) QPIP employee premiums on your Current or projected pre-retirement salary + bonus -- the other normal deductions that come off a paycheque besides income tax. 📈 Accumulation

Projects your savings forward from today to your chosen retirement age, across your RRSP, TFSA, Non-Registered, and (if applicable) LIF accounts, using each account's own growth-rate assumption plus your entered contributions. The balances this phase lands on become the starting balances the Retirement Income tab's simulation begins from.

  • TFSA room starts from an assumed $20,000 of existing room today and accrues the national TFSA dollar limit of new room every year, independent of income -- $7,000 for 2026, and since 2026-09-17 indexed forward at CPI minus the haircut and rounded to the nearest $500 the way the real limit is (it used to stay a flat $7,000 for the whole horizon).
  • RRSP room accrual is described under Income and Budget above; it's tracked all the way through the Accumulation phase, then carried into the Retirement Income tab purely for display (the RRSP/RRIF hover box) -- it stops growing once you retire, since RRSP contributions stop and the RRSP converts to a RRIF.

Target vs. Budget vs. Cash Flow

This app tracks two independent, separately-set spending figures, and it's important not to conflate them. Target Income (the "Target" column in the Year-by-Year table, editable by clicking any year's amount) is a simple year-by-year figure -- it defaults to flat example amounts by age ($82,000 under 65, stepping down to $55,000 by 80) unless you edit it. Its only job is to size how much extra your LIF/RRIF draw that year goes beyond the legal minimum, as described in Draw-down order below. Budget (the "Budget" column, driven entirely by your real, granular line items on the Income and Budget tab) is the actual number the plan compares your income against to decide whether a year is a surplus (deposited to TFSA/Non-Registered) or a shortfall (drawn from TFSA/Non-Registered/Emergency Fund) -- exactly what the Cash Flow column shows.

Because these two figures are set independently, they routinely disagree in direction -- a year can show a Target "shortfall" while your real Budget-driven Cash Flow is comfortably positive, or the reverse. That's expected, not a bug: Target only ever decides registered-account sizing, never the surplus/shortfall outcome itself. Draw-down order

Every year, the plan first totals up your mandatory income -- DB pension, the LIF's minimum withdrawal, the RRIF's minimum withdrawal, QPP/CPP, and OAS (net of tax and any OAS clawback on that mandatory-only total). If that's not enough to cover your Target Income (see Target vs. Budget vs. Cash Flow above -- this is the Target figure only, not your real Budget), the plan draws an extra amount from the LIF and RRIF beyond their minimums, split between the two in proportion to their balances -- but the LIF's extra draw is capped at its own annual maximum (see the next subsection); anything the LIF legally can't pay is redirected to the RRIF instead, since the RRIF has no maximum.

2026-09-14 correction: a calculation review found that extra amount was previously SIZED using an assumed flat "keep rate" (roughly 70-72 cents surviving tax and OAS clawback per extra dollar withdrawn, regardless of your real bracket, province, or how close you already were to the OAS clawback threshold), and was capped at $130,000 of extra withdrawal a year regardless of how much room your accounts actually had. Both are now real: the plan searches for the exact extra gross withdrawal that nets your real spending target after running it through the same real tax-and-clawback calculation the rest of this app uses, bounded only by your accounts' real legal withdrawal room (the LIF's own maximum, and however much your RRIF actually holds) -- not an arbitrary dollar ceiling. In most years the difference from the old assumption is small; it's largest in high-income years or years close to the OAS clawback threshold, where a flat 70-72% assumption diverges furthest from your real marginal rate.

2026-09-17 (review follow-up): the "top up to Target" search now evaluates tax with the same pension income amount credit (and OAS exclusion) the year's own tax uses, so net income lands on the Target instead of overshooting it by the credit's value (up to about $280 a year from 65).

If mandatory-plus-extra income still isn't enough to cover the target, or if it's more than enough, the plan routes the difference through your remaining accounts in a fixed priority order, chosen deliberately rather than arbitrarily. Note this routing step compares your income against your real Budget need (Income and Budget tab), not the Target figure above -- see Target vs. Budget vs. Cash Flow. This is the step that actually produces the Cash Flow column's Surplus/Shortfall:

  • Surplus (income exceeds your real Budget need): in Resident mode, the extra is deposited into your TFSA first (up to whatever room you have), then any leftover into your Non-Registered account. In Non-Resident mode, surplus goes straight to Non-Registered, bypassing the TFSA -- a non-resident generally can't hold or contribute to a TFSA the same way.
  • Shortfall (income falls short of your real Budget need): the gap is drawn from your Non-Registered account first, then your TFSA, and only once both of those are fully drained does the plan touch your Emergency Fund as a last resort. The reasoning: Non-Registered money already carries ongoing tax drag and is fully taxed as a capital gain at death, so there's little reason to preserve it; a TFSA compounds and can be withdrawn tax-free, so it's worth preserving longer.

Any manual override you've set on a specific year's RRIF or TFSA withdrawal (via the Year-by-Year table) takes precedence over this automatic routing for that year. If any shortfall remains after all four accounts are exhausted, it shows up in the Year-by-Year table as an actual funding gap for that year.

RRSP meltdown

A real, commonly-used Canadian tax-planning strategy, added 2026-09-13: the "🔥 RRSP Meltdown" checkbox (Canada Resident only, next to "50% LIF→RRIF" in the toolbar) deliberately draws more than the RRIF's mandatory minimum in your earlier retirement years -- topping up that year's total taxable income to the ceiling of whichever federal bracket the dropdown next to it selects (e.g. "to 20.5% bracket") -- rather than only ever drawing extra to cover a spending shortfall the way the draw-down order above does. The idea: shrink the RRIF balance now, while you're still in a lower bracket, ahead of the mandatory-minimum percentage climbing steeply with age -- trading some tax paid sooner for less tax, and less OAS clawback exposure, later.

It's only active from your retirement age through the last year before both your QPP/CPP and OAS have started -- once both benefits are running, the case for drawing extra weakens, so the meltdown automatically stops and the plan reverts to its normal draw. The bracket ceiling itself is indexed forward every year the same way every other bracket figure in this app is (see the live tax-bracket table in the Tax Breakdown section below). A manual RRIF withdrawal override you've set for a specific year (via the Year-by-Year table) always takes precedence over the meltdown for that year -- the meltdown only ever tops up a year that has no such override. Scope: Canada Resident mode, RRSP/RRIF only -- it never touches the LIF.

LIF and RRIF minimum & maximum withdrawals

Both the LIF and the RRIF (and the RRSP, once converted) have a minimum withdrawal required by law every year, based on your age and that account's opening balance -- up to age 70 it's the formula 1 ÷ (90 − age) (4.00% at 65, 5.00% at 70), and from 71 onward it follows the government's published prescribed-factor table exactly (5.28% at 71, rising to 20% at 95 and over) -- until 2026-09-16 the ages between the table's five-year milestones were interpolated in straight lines, overstating the forced minimum by up to two-thirds of a point in the late 80s and early 90s. Only the LIF additionally has a maximum -- a second, higher percentage you're not allowed to withdraw beyond in a given year (outside of a one-time unlocking provision) -- because a LIF exists specifically to guarantee you an income for life, unlike an RRSP/RRIF which has no such ceiling. Modeling assumption (noted 2026-09-17): the RRSP is treated as converted to a RRIF at your retirement age, so its mandatory minimum starts then (e.g. 2.86% at 55), not in the year after you turn 71 as the law actually requires -- a retiree living on Non-Reg/TFSA/DB until 71 can't yet model leaving the RRSP untouched, and in surplus years the forced draw simply flows, after tax, into Non-Reg.

Quebec eliminated its LIF maximum for ages 55 and up (effective January 1, 2025) -- once you're 55, there's no regulatory ceiling on annual LIF withdrawals in Quebec. Ontario, British Columbia, and Alberta instead use a shared annuity-based formula (keyed to a floor long-term-bond rate, currently 6%). If your LIF is federally regulated (the "Federally regulated pension" checkbox), a separate federal table applies instead of the provincial one. Any LIF can also be fully unlocked (its maximum waived entirely, 100% withdrawable) once its balance drops low enough relative to that year's Year's Maximum Pensionable Earnings (YMPE) -- the exact threshold and minimum age differ by jurisdiction (federal: at most 50% of YMPE, any age; Ontario: under 40% of YMPE, age 55+; BC/Alberta: under 40% of YMPE at 65+, under 20% below 65).

QPP/CPP and OAS

Quebec's government retirement pension is called QPP; every other province's is CPP -- same benefit, different name, no numeric difference modeled here. Both QPP/CPP and OAS can be deferred past age 65 in exchange for a permanently larger monthly amount, and both apply the OAS clawback (a 15% recovery tax on net income above a yearly-indexed threshold, capped at the OAS amount itself) independently every year. Starting QPP/CPP before 65 (the 0.6%-per-month reduction down to age 60) is not modeled -- the slider floor is 65 -- so a "take it at 60" comparison has to be done outside this planner (noted 2026-09-17).

Auto-adjustment when you change your retirement age: if you pick a retirement age later than your currently-set QPP/CPP or OAS start age, that start age is automatically pushed forward to match your new retirement age (capped at that benefit's own latest allowed start age) -- the reasoning being that you generally can't be drawing a pension before you've actually retired. If you later pick an earlier retirement age again, your original preferred start age is restored. QPP/CPP and OAS are adjusted independently of each other, and this never pushes a start age earlier than what you'd actually chosen.

DB pension

If you have a defined-benefit pension, you enter its expected annual amount at a handful of checkpoint ages (55, 60, 61, 62, 63, 64, 65) -- the plan uses whichever checkpoint is the nearest one at or below your retirement age. Between ages 65 and your chosen "freeze age" (default 75), the pension compounds forward each year at a COLA (cost-of-living adjustment) rate you set (default 1%/yr); from the freeze age onward, it's held flat at whatever it had grown to. A separate bridge benefit -- a flat dollar amount, typically representing the portion of a DB pension paid only until government benefits start -- can be added on top of the regular pension amount, automatically dropping to $0 once you reach its end age (default 65).

Emergency Fund

The Budget tab's "Emergency fund" savings line becomes a dedicated account once you're retired. Each year, the plan checks whether its balance already covers 6 months of that year's budgeted living expenses; if it does, that year's contribution is skipped (and the skipped dollars flow instead into the normal surplus routing above, as if they were never earmarked for the fund). This check runs fresh every year, not just once -- if inflation later grows your living expenses past what the fund (now static) covers, contributions automatically resume. The Emergency Fund grows at its own fixed, conservative rate (1.5%/yr) regardless of your other accounts' settings, and -- as described in Draw-down order above -- is only ever drawn from as an absolute last resort, after both your Non-Registered account and TFSA are fully exhausted.

Asset conversion

An "Asset" (e.g. a home) you've entered converts into retirement income exactly once, in the first simulated year at or after both its own "Converts to income at age" setting and your retirement age (whichever is later) -- not automatically at a fixed rule. Its value is grown forward at its own entered growth rate up to that conversion year. If the asset is marked tax-free (e.g. a principal residence), the full grown value becomes income; otherwise it's treated as taxable Canadian property, with only the growth since today counted as a capital gain (taxed at the usual 50% inclusion rate, at that year's marginal tax rate). Either way, the net proceeds always land in your Non-Registered account. An asset whose conversion age falls beyond your simulated horizon still counts toward your Net Estate at its projected value, so it's never simply left out.

Non-Resident mode

Choosing Non-Resident/Expat switches to a different simulation engine and tax treatment entirely. Canadian-source pension, RRIF/LIF, dividend, and interest income are each subject to non-resident withholding tax -- defaulting to Canada's plain statutory 25% for all three, editable if your actual treaty rate differs. For pension/RRIF/LIF income specifically, the plan compares that flat withholding against the alternative Section 217 election every single year -- full graduated federal tax brackets plus the basic personal amount credit, then a 48% surtax "in lieu of provincial tax" -- and automatically uses whichever produces less tax that year, exactly as a real S.217 election would.

A separate "LIF-transfer election" controls what happens to your LIF balance as a non-resident: None (default) leaves it alone, drawing at the federal LIF maximum like any other account; Half permanently moves 50% of your starting LIF balance into your RRIF up front (a tax-free registered-to-registered transfer), so that half then follows ordinary RRIF rules (minimum only, no maximum) for the rest of retirement; Full keeps the LIF drawing at the federal maximum for a mandatory 2-year non-residency waiting period, then redeems the entire remaining balance as a one-time lump sum in year 3, with the after-tax proceeds landing in your Non-Registered account and the LIF empty from then on. That year-3 lump is withheld at the statutory 25% Part XIII rate for lump-sum payments (since 2026-09-17), not at the editable periodic-pension rate from the Profile tab -- treaties that lower the periodic rate almost never lower the lump-sum one.

Net Estate

The Net Estate figure (and each account's own line in the estate grid) reflects each account's real closing balance at the end of the very last simulated year -- the same growth, on the same amount, that every other year in the simulation already applies, using whichever rate actually governed that final year: your flat assumption, a per-year rate override, a Realistic Non-Reg tax composition, or (while Monte Carlo or the Reverse Sequence Risk Test is active) that run's own randomized return. The Non-Registered account's death tax is calculated for real, from that account's actual adjusted cost base (ACB) at that same final closing point -- 50% of the unrealized gain, taxed at your marginal rate that year (Resident mode only; Non-Residents owe $0 here, since publicly-traded securities are excluded from "taxable Canadian property"). An asset (e.g. a home) whose conversion age falls beyond your simulated horizon still counts toward Net Estate at its own projected value, even though it never appears inside the year-by-year rows. Not modeled (noted 2026-09-17): the capital-gains tax on such a not-yet-converted asset (it is counted at full value), any OAS recovery tax triggered on the final return by the registered balance being added to that year's income, and probate or estate-administration fees (Ontario's EAT is 1.5% above $50,000; Quebec notarial wills are exempt).

Funds Run Out vs. Portfolio Depleted

Portfolio Depleted is purely informational -- it tells you when your invested accounts hit $0, which is worth knowing (from that point on you're leaning entirely on guaranteed income, with no cushion left for an unplanned expense) but is never itself a failure. Monte Carlo & Historical Bootstrap

Instead of assuming your portfolio grows at one flat rate every year, this optional tool re-runs your plan hundreds of times with a different randomized sequence of yearly returns each time, then shows the spread of outcomes (e.g. a 10th-to-90th-percentile band around your Net Estate) -- a way of seeing how much your plan's success depends on the market cooperating, not just on the averages. Returns can be drawn either from a statistical distribution (using your own entered mean and standard deviation) or bootstrapped from blocks of real historical S&P 500 returns, including an optional blended equity/bond mix.

The headline success rate ("X% of Y simulated retirements were fully funded") counts only real funding shortfalls -- see "Funds Run Out vs. Portfolio Depleted" above. A separate, independent depletion rate is also shown whenever it's above 0%: the share of iterations where your invested accounts reached $0 at some point, whether or not that iteration was otherwise a full success.

Reverse Sequence Risk Test

A companion to Monte Carlo, added 2026-09-13. Rather than random sampling, it takes one fixed, historically-grounded "bad stretch" -- the actual 1929 market crash and its aftermath, standardized to your own plan's mean and standard deviation so it reflects your plan's real shape rather than a generic illustration -- and runs your plan through that exact sequence of returns twice: once forward (the crash hits early in retirement) and once in reverse (the same returns, but the crash hits late). Comparing the two isolates the effect of sequence-of-returns risk specifically -- the same average return, delivered in a different order, can produce a meaningfully different outcome, and how much it matters for you depends on how hard your plan is drawing against your portfolio relative to its size.

Each of the forward and reverse runs shows its own "ran out" age (a real funding shortfall, if any) and, separately, its own "portfolio depleted" age (accounts reaching $0, if any) -- see "Funds Run Out vs. Portfolio Depleted" above. Realistic Non-Reg tax mode composes with this test the same way it now does with Monte Carlo -- see the 2026-09-14 correction under "Monte Carlo & Historical Bootstrap" above.

For a risk-based range instead, run Monte Carlo or the Reverse Sequence Risk Test further down this tab -- results aren't cached here, precisely to avoid these cards ever showing a stale range from a run you've since changed the plan out from under.

Sustainable Spend Scenario

A toggleable what-if overlay, added 2026-09-13, that answers a different question than the rest of the planner does: not "does my current Budget hold up?" but "how much could I safely spend?" Click "🔍 Find sustainable rate" (in the card just below the age-summary strip) and it searches for the highest uniform scale on your overall Budget-tab spending -- every expense group's dollar total, multiplied by one factor -- that still passes a strict viability bar every single year through the end of your horizon: a deterministic pass with zero shortfall, and a Reverse Sequence Risk Test that stays fully funded in both the forward and reversed 1929-shaped crash orderings. Debt repayment is deliberately excluded from the scaling -- a debt payment isn't discretionary spending you'd actually cut back on to make a plan sustainable, so it's left at its real scheduled amount either way. Viability here means zero real funding shortfall -- see "Funds Run Out vs. Portfolio Depleted" above; if the sustainable rate found still depletes your invested accounts before the end of your horizon (while remaining fully funded via guaranteed income), the result shows a note telling you at what age that happens.

This is a genuinely different lever from the per-year Target Income table (Year-by-Year table, click a year's Target cell): Target Income only controls how aggressively the LIF/RRIF draw beyond their mandatory minimums (affecting tax timing and which account gets drawn from), and has no effect on whether the plan runs out of money. Whether a plan is viable is driven entirely by your real Income & Budget tab spending -- so this tool searches that instead.

The result shows the sustainable rate as a percentage of your current Budget spending (and its dollar equivalent at the start of retirement), whether your current spending was already viable or needed to come down, and a Net Estate comparison against your current plan. Clicking "Activate this rate" turns the overlay on for your live simulation -- every chart, table, and card on this tab (and the Tax Breakdown/Report tabs) reflects the scaled spending until you click "Deactivate", exactly like the Monte Carlo or Reverse Sequence Risk Test overlays. Your actual Budget tab numbers are never changed -- this only affects the simulation while the overlay is active, the same way a what-if toggle should. It applies equally in Resident and Non-Resident mode, and only from your retirement age onward (pre-retirement projections on the Accumulation tab are untouched).

Added 2026-09-15: a second search shape, Go-Go/Slow-Go/No-Go, alongside the original uniform scale -- pick either one with the radio buttons in the card before clicking "Find sustainable rate". Instead of one flat multiplier for your whole retirement, the phased shape applies the found scale at 100% during your Go-Go years, then tapers to 85% during Slow-Go and 70% during No-Go -- the same age bands (74/84) used by the phase cards above -- reflecting the well-documented real-world pattern of retirees spending more on travel and discretionary activities early in retirement and less as they age. The two modes are searched and activated independently (finding a rate under one doesn't change the other), and only one can be active at a time. The phase ratios are fixed and not user-editable, by design -- they're a deliberately conservative rule of thumb, not a personalized forecast. As with the uniform mode, this doesn't model any specific late-life cost increase (e.g. healthcare or long-term care) -- it only tapers discretionary Budget-tab spending down; if you expect a specific late-life cost increase, model it directly in the Budget tab for those years instead.

Add to Compare

The "🆚 Add to Compare" button, just below the age-summary strip, saves the scenario you're currently viewing straight into the Compare tab without switching there first -- it's the exact same save as the Compare tab's own "💾 Save current plan" button, just reachable from here. 🧾 Tax (a Retirement Income sub-tab)

A year-by-year table of taxable income, federal tax, provincial tax (or, in Non-Resident mode, whichever withholding method was used), total tax, and marginal/effective rates -- for whichever scenario (mode, retirement age, QPP/OAS start ages) is currently selected. These figures come straight from the same tax-calculation functions the simulation itself uses to decide how much you actually receive each year, so this tab can never disagree with the rest of the app.

Clicking a row expands it into full detail: for Resident mode, side-by-side federal and provincial bracket-by-bracket tables showing exactly which slice of income was taxed at which rate, the basic personal amount credit, the age amount and pension income amount credit described below, Quebec's 16.5% federal abatement where applicable, and Ontario's two-tier surtax where applicable. For Non-Resident mode, the flat-withholding calculation and the Section 217 election are shown side by side, with whichever one the simulation actually used that year marked. Every expanded row also shows a per-account table -- LIF, RRIF, TFSA, Non-Registered -- with each account's opening balance, the amount withdrawn, the withdrawal as a percentage of opening balance, and that account's actual realized rate of return for the year.

Reference: current federal and provincial tax brackets (live -- reflects whichever province is selected on the Profile tab and today's dollar figures before any yearly escalation).

How the plan's arithmetic is checked

Added 2026-09-20 (N13). The register above checks each constant against its published source. Whether those constants are assembled correctly is a separate question, and tests/132-reference-plans.js is what answers it. Six reference plans -- a plain single-person retirement; a Quebec plan with QPP, a DB pension and a LIF; an Ontario couple of different ages retiring on different dates; a high income running into the OAS clawback; an early death with a surviving spouse; and an asset sale with a final estate return -- are run end to end and reconciled against calculations made outside the app. The statutory structure is recovered from behaviour: bracket rates are read off the slope of the tax function and compared with the published tables, RRIF factors are recovered from the minimum actually withdrawn, the LIF maximum is recomputed from the annuity formula, and the 15% clawback, the 50% capital-gains inclusion and the 42%/36% deferrals are recomputed from the statute. Then closed-form identities are checked for every year of every plan: each account closes at (opening − withdrawn + deposited) × (1 + its own rate), one year's closing balances are the next year's opening balances, taxable income is exactly DB + LIF + RRIF + QPP + OAS, cash in equals cash out, and the estate is the sum of its own parts, each net of its own tax.

What this does not establish: it is not equivalence with a commercial planner. No such product was consulted, and a figure out of one could not be derived, so a difference could be noted but not explained. It also does not re-prove the constants themselves -- that is the register above, deliberately. Its expectations are written as arithmetic rather than as frozen dollar figures, so next January's indexation moves them along with the constants instead of against them.

Separately, if you use the Accumulation tab's opt-in "Realistic Non-Reg tax" toggle with Canadian eligible dividends in your account composition, Quebec's dividend tax credit was coded at a stale 11.78% -- Revenu Québec's own current filing instructions (line 415) give 16.146% of the grossed-up dividend, so dividend income held this way was previously taxed somewhat higher than it actually would be. 2026-09-14 correction (age amount & pension income amount): every "Less other credits" line on this tab used to be a single assumed $2,200 placeholder, applied the same way regardless of your age or how much of your income actually came from a pension. It's now two real, eligibility-based Canadian tax credits. The age amount (federal line 30100; Quebec's ligne 361; Ontario's own equivalent) only applies once you turn 65, and shrinks as your income rises past a threshold, fully disappearing at a high enough income. The pension income amount (federal line 31400) credits up to $2,000 of "eligible pension income" -- a defined-benefit pension counts at any age, but LIF/RRIF withdrawals only count once you turn 65; QPP/CPP, OAS, and GIS are never eligible, no matter your age. Unlike almost every other dollar figure in this app, the federal $2,000 cap is deliberately left un-indexed going forward, matching the fact that the CRA has held it at $2,000, unchanged, since 1988; Ontario's own pension amount, by contrast, is indexed forward, so the two credits drift further apart from each other over a long retirement. Quebec combines its age amount and pension-equivalent amount into one base, reduced together by a single income-tested formula, rather than as two independent credits -- reconstructed here from Revenu Québec's published parameters as this app's best approximation, not a line-by-line replica of their official worksheet. British Columbia and Alberta (kept in the code but not currently selectable) don't yet have this credit modeled and correctly show $0 for it rather than guessing.

🆚 Compare

Lets you save your current plan's results into up to four named scenario slots, then compare them side by side with a stats table (Net Estate, Total Lifetime Tax, when/whether funds run out, when/whether the portfolio is depleted, average withdrawal rate, retirement age) and an overlaid balance-by-age chart. Each scenario is a snapshot taken at the moment you click "Save" -- not a live link back to your current plan -- so if you change an assumption afterward (a tax rate, a retirement age, an account balance), a saved scenario will not update on its own; click "Update with current plan" on that slot to refresh it. This is deliberate: it lets you freely keep experimenting with your current settings without a saved comparison scenario silently changing underneath you. You can also save straight from the Retirement Income tab with its "🆚 Add to Compare" button -- see that subsection above.
